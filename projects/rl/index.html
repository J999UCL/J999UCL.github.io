3D Connect 4

Reinforcement learning · C++ · PyTorch

I built a self-play reinforcement learning agent for gravity-based 4×4×4 Connect 4. There are 16 columns to play into, and a winning line can run horizontally, vertically, or diagonally through the cube. I wanted the bot to learn strong play rather than rely entirely on hand-written rules.

Search and self-play

I started with a heuristic opponent that took immediate wins, blocked threats, and avoided obvious traps. This provided training examples and a baseline I could play against through a CLI. I then implemented an AlphaZero-style loop: a policy/value network guides Monte Carlo tree search, self-play generates positions, and the network learns from the search policies and game outcomes.

The training objective combines cross-entropy against the MCTS visit distribution with a value loss against the final outcome. The game and PUCT search run in C++, using bitboards; PyTorch trains the network.

The cost of looking ahead

Search made each training position expensive. In the initial single-threaded, unbatched benchmark, increasing the budget from 32 to 800 simulations per move reduced generation from 18.61 to 1.52 positions per second. Those measurements used a small, randomly initialized network: they measure computational cost, not playing strength.

I explored parallelism within the search tree and across independent games. Shared-tree search uses virtual loss to spread workers across branches, while a batched TorchScript inference service lets several games share GPU evaluations.

Data diversity and symmetry

Head-to-head improvements did not tell the whole story. Inspecting the empty-board policy revealed a strong preference for one corner, even though the four corners are equivalent under the board’s symmetries. Diversifying the seed positions and adding symmetry augmentation made the opening distribution much more balanced.

Evaluating improvement

I compared checkpoints with their predecessors, the starting model, and depth-limited alpha-beta opponents. These tests sometimes disagreed: a checkpoint could improve against the starting model while failing the promotion threshold against its predecessor. This made a single rolling opponent an unreliable summary of progress.

I also built a DAG-based oracle solver that reuses solved positions and caches move evaluations. It provided a way to assess decisions against solved or partially solved positions, rather than relying exclusively on another learned opponent.

Curriculum and training direction

The final part of the presentation compares expensive MCTS-generated targets with faster actor-critic self-play, and considers an endgame-first curriculum. Starting near the end of a game gives shorter paths to a reward; training can then move back toward openings as competence improves.

The project taught me to treat data generation, policy diagnostics, and evaluation as part of the learning algorithm. More search per move has a cost, beating the last checkpoint is an incomplete test, and diverse training positions matter as much as the network architecture.

Additional result: tactical pretraining

The supervised starting model achieved 93.7% top-1 target accuracy on 100,000 held-out tactical positions. Immediate-win and immediate-block accuracy exceeded 98%, while agreement with depth-three minimax targets was 69.6%. This measures agreement with curriculum labels, not match win rate.
